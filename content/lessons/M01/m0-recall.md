# Recall bridge: how this course works, and the arithmetic you need

This is the first lesson of the mathematics track, so there is nothing earlier to review. Instead, this movement explains how every lesson works and checks the handful of arithmetic facts the lesson leans on. If anything here feels shaky, slow down now rather than later.

## The shape of every lesson

Every lesson is a long arc of eight movements, and each one is a habit that Richard Feynman described when he explained how he learned physics:

1. **Recall.** Cards from earlier lessons come back at the moment you need them. Forgetting is normal; retrieving what you half-forgot is what makes it stick.
2. **Puzzle.** You meet something surprising and commit to a prediction before any definition appears. The name of a thing comes last; the thing comes first.
3. **Derivation.** You build every result from earlier ones in small steps, filling in the blanks yourself. Nothing is stated that you have not made.
4. **Build.** You write the object from scratch in Python. "What I cannot create, I do not understand."
5. **Scale.** You estimate, then measure, at realistic sizes.
6. **Break it.** You break your own construction on purpose and predict each failure before you run it. "You must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool."
7. **Feynman.** You explain the idea in plain words to someone younger. Where your explanation goes vague, you have found a gap.
8. **Capstone.** One larger project uses everything.

Theory and practice each take exactly half of the lesson's time.

## Slow and steady

A lesson here is ten or twelve hours: about eight sessions of ninety minutes over ten to fourteen days. The app holds you to that pace with four rules.

- One lesson is in progress at a time. A lesson unlocks when the lessons it builds on are complete and their plain-language explanations have passed.
- Each session starts with your due review cards. A new movement opens only when they are cleared.
- A lesson closes for good only when, a week after you finish it, you answer at least 80 percent of its review cards correctly.
- After about ninety minutes, the next movement boundary suggests a break.

Your review cards are a notebook of things you do not yet know well. Wrong predictions and weak spots come back until they are not weak any more.

## The arithmetic this lesson uses

Four facts carry most of this lesson. Each blank below takes a number or a short expression; press the check button after each.

::::derivation{id=d0 title="Warm-up: powers of ten, fractions and doubling"}
:::step
A thousand is $10^3$ and ten thousand is $10^4$. Multiplying them, $10^3 \times 10^4 = 10^{\,?}$: the exponent is [[7]], because you are counting zeros.
:::
:::step
Fractions add over a common denominator: $\dfrac{3}{4} + \dfrac{1}{6} = \dfrac{9}{12} + \dfrac{2}{12} =$ [[11/12]].
:::
:::step
Dividing by a fraction multiplies by its flip: $\dfrac{1}{0.25} =$ [[4]].
:::
:::step
Doubling ten times gives $2^{10} =$ [[1024]], which is close to $10^3$. Remember this one: it converts between powers of two (how computers count) and powers of ten (how people count).
:::
::::

If those came easily, you are ready. In this lesson numbers grow very large (a trillion operations) and very small (a millionth of a second), and the main skill is keeping track of how many zeros you are carrying.
